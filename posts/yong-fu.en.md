Yong Fu (甬府) is a Ningbo restaurant holding one MICHELIN star in the MICHELIN Guide Shanghai 2026 and three Diamonds from Black Pearl. The character "甬" is the short name for the city of Ningbo. The branch I visited is North Bund, which opened in September 2022 as the flagship of the Yong Fu group. It sits on the 56th floor of the East Tower of North Bund Raffles on Dongdaming Road, looking straight across to Pudong, with river views from the private rooms and an open kitchen. This branch has held its Michelin star since the 2023 guide, its very first year in the guide.

The dining room is dressed in marble and chandeliers, with seven private rooms.

The Yong Fu brand was founded by Chef Weng Yongjun (翁拥军), a Ningbo native who started out as a hotel kitchen apprentice and climbed all the way to hotel general manager, before striking out on his own to open the first Yong Fu in Shanghai in 2011. He wanted a restaurant that "had ideas of its own", free of the constraints that had held him back before. His belief was simple: if Cantonese food could sell for 1,000 yuan a head, so could Ningbo food. Yong Fu earned its first Michelin star in 2017 and has kept it ever since.

Today the group runs three fine-dining restaurants in Shanghai and has expanded to Hong Kong and Singapore, and the Hong Kong outpost also features in Tatler Best 20 Restaurants Hong Kong 2026.

His principle is "never cook other people's dishes" (不做别人的菜), and he buys produce without haggling: "if we see something good and they dare to name the price, we buy it." The North Bund branch is led by Chef Xu Kun Lei (徐昆磊), a Ningbo native with more than 30 years in the kitchen, who reinterprets Ningbo cooking for today with contemporary ingredients. The chef places particular emphasis on the quality of the seafood, with fresh deliveries every day. The menu is split half and half between classic Ningbo dishes and creative ones, and that is what makes this restaurant stand out.

For the uninitiated, Ningbo cooking has a flavour profile of 咸鲜, salty and fresh, quite unlike the 浓油赤酱, rich oil and dark, sweet soy, of true Shanghainese cooking, which Thai diners often confuse. Ningbo food is defined by "鲜咸合一", freshness and saltiness as one. Salt is the main seasoning, not sugar; soy and other seasoning sauces are used sparingly, in the belief that they mask the taste of the ingredients; and fermented and preserved ingredients such as fermented soybean paste are used generously.

True to the concept, the produce here is not drawn from one place alone; the best is gathered from many sources. The mantis shrimp come from Ningbo, the squid from the East China Sea (东海), the scallops from Dalian, and the soft-fleshed pomfret from Ningbo.

Overall, for me, Yong Fu blends authentic Ningbo cooking with a gentle touch of creativity, so that even those unfamiliar with this cuisine can open up to it with ease. It is a truly worthy ambassador for Ningbo cuisine.

The highlight of this meal was the pigeon leg wrapped around crab meat, a new dish that I think could easily become the restaurant's icon. Another favourite was the signature marinated crab, so delicious that we had to order more.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Ningbo Marinated Crab (宁式十八斩) 🕶️
image: ./images/yong-fu/01w.jpg | Raw marinated swimming crab cut into pieces and arranged in a ring in a dark sauce, on a blue-and-white porcelain plate, Yong Fu, Shanghai
Ningbo's most iconic dish, and the true test of a kitchen, because it demands crab of exceptional freshness. It uses three-spot swimming crab (梭子蟹), cured raw in brine without ever touching heat, cut into 18 pieces as the name says, then dressed with sauce. Today it was male crab, which is better than the female at this time of year. The flesh is translucent and viscous, almost like jelly, yet slips down smoothly, with no fishiness at all and no briny smell of the sea. The meat is delicately sweet, its freshness locked in by freezing on board the boat. The sauce is complex, with ginger, vinegar and a gentle touch of chilli. A truly excellent dish, well worthy of being the chef's signature.

### Fruitwood-Smoked Stone Frog Legs (果木熏石蛙腿)
image: ./images/yong-fu/02.jpg | Smoked stone frog legs glazed in an amber sauce and arranged in a star, with greens in the centre, Yong Fu, Shanghai
Stone frog legs smoked over fruitwood. 石蛙, the Chinese spiny frog, lives in mountain streams in Zhejiang and Fujian, though most today are farmed. The texture is finer than chicken and the meat is tender. The smoke is never too aggressive, just a light, sweet smokiness, with a gentle, salty umami. The skin isn't tough, and the smoking is just right.

### Crispy Fried Hairtail Belly (香酥带鱼腩)
image: ./images/yong-fu/03.jpg | Crispy fried hairtail belly in an orange-red sauce, dusted with white powder, on a blue-and-white porcelain plate, Yong Fu, Shanghai
The chef uses only the belly of 带鱼, silver hairtail, from the East China Sea, especially the Zhoushan (舟山) area, regarded as the finest grade of hairtail for Chinese cooking, and fries it. Nicely done: the flesh is light and fluffy, the fish rich and oily, and the sauce leans sweet.

### Beef Tripe in Chilli Oil (红油绣球毛肚)
image: ./images/yong-fu/04.jpg | Beef tripe rolled into balls topped with caviar, sitting in red chilli oil, Yong Fu, Shanghai
From the "creative" half of the menu, inspired by Sichuan chilli oil. It uses 毛肚, beef tripe, scored in a grid so it curls into a ball (绣球) with more surface to catch the sauce, filled with mushrooms and vegetables and topped with caviar. Crunchy with no gaminess, a gentle warmth from the chilli oil, and the caviar only very lightly salty.

### Roasted Taro in Soy Sauce (酱烧芋艿头)
image: ./images/yong-fu/05.jpg | Small taro roasted in dark soy sauce, piled high and topped with green shoots, in a red-and-white patterned bowl, Yong Fu, Shanghai
Made with Fenghua taro (奉化芋艿头), a prized Zhejiang speciality whose starchy flesh is fine and crumbly yet sticky, harvested in autumn and right in season now. The people of Ningbo eat it from the Mid-Autumn Festival into early winter. Here the taro is wonderfully soft, naturally sweet in the flesh, with a gentle note of soy.

### Pickled Winter Melon with Sesame Oil (香油腌冬瓜)
image: ./images/yong-fu/06.jpg | Cubes of pickled winter melon drizzled with sesame oil, scattered with tiny coriander leaves, in a blue-and-white porcelain bowl, Yong Fu, Shanghai
Salt-cured winter melon dressed with sesame oil: the restaurant's more approachable take on 臭冬瓜, fermented winter melon. It is one of Ningbo's "three stinkies" (三臭), namely winter melon, amaranth stems and taro stems, which local households boil and then steep in an old fermenting brine kept going over the years, to eat with rice porridge in summer. Here it is only lightly pickled so the flavour isn't heavy: sour first, cooling, dressed with white sesame oil, with the scent of sesame at the tip of the nose.

### Stonefish Stewed with Porcupinefish (石头鱼炖刺豚)
image: ./images/yong-fu/07.jpg | Thick white soup with stonefish and porcupinefish skin, sprinkled with chopped Chinese celery, in a white porcelain bowl, Yong Fu, Shanghai
One of the signature dishes of Ningbo cooking. It uses stonefish (石头鱼), which has venomous spines but firm, white, highly gelatinous flesh, together with the skin and fins of porcupinefish (刺豚), a different species from the poisonous fugu pufferfish. The broth turns thick and white on its own from the collagen, with no cream needed. The stonefish is used live and served with both skin and flesh.
The soup is creamy and white, seasoned with salt, with Chinese celery and plenty of umami, and not a trace of fishiness. The skin is bouncy, soft and a little crunchy, not as chewy and crisp as the Japanese style. Delicious.

### Dalian Scallop with Ningbo Fermented Bean Paste (甬味土酱獐子岛元贝)
image: ./images/yong-fu/08w.jpg | Fried scallop under a crisp shell-shaped wafer, dressed with fermented bean sauce, with rings of green pickled bamboo shoot around the plate, Yong Fu, Shanghai
Today's scallops come from Zhangzi Island (獐子岛) in the Yellow Sea, near Dalian, where Japanese scallops are farmed. They come with a Ningbo-style fermented bean paste the restaurant ferments itself for a month, served in a faux shell made from a crisp floral wafer, with pickled bamboo shoots on the side.
The scallops here are every bit as good as the finest from Japan, sweet and firm. The battered, fried scallop is very large and cooked just right; I suspect it was cooked sous vide first and then fried. The flesh is very sweet and very tender. The sauce is salty and full of umami, with a faint scent of fermented bean paste and a light touch of fried garlic, while the pickled bamboo shoots, a little spicy and a little sour, cut through it nicely. This dish paired beautifully with a Chardonnay from Yunnan.

### Crispy Roe-Laden Mantis Shrimp with Lychee-Scented Fermented Bean Sauce (荔枝阴豆瓣脆皮膏虾菇)
image: ./images/yong-fu/09.jpg | Crispy fried roe-laden mantis shrimp cut in half to show the flesh inside, on a red fermented bean sauce, Yong Fu, Shanghai
Made with female mantis shrimp carrying roe, the "膏" being the most prized and delicious part.
The batter is light and airy, and the mantis shrimp half-cooked, half-raw: excellent flesh, jelly-like and sweet. It is served with a fermented bean chilli sauce that leads with heat; the fermented note barely comes through, and there is a fruity sweet-sour edge, rather like sweet-and-sour sauce mixed with spicy bean paste.

### Baked Pomfret with Spring Onion and Ginger (葱汁姜茸焗鲳鱼)
image: ./images/yong-fu/10.jpg | Baked pomfret topped with spring onion and ginger, on diced potato and lengths of spring onion, Yong Fu, Shanghai
White pomfret from Zhoushan, one of Ningbo's celebrated delicacies, with white, tender, flaky flesh. Baked for 3 to 5 minutes at 180°C, which leaves the flesh beautifully soft. Gently salty with no sauce, its richness cut by grated ginger, with sweetness from the roasted spring onion and a sharp bite from the fresh. Diced potato sits underneath.

### Snow Crab with Pickled Water Bamboo and Glass Noodles (酸茭白松叶蟹粉丝)
image: ./images/yong-fu/11.jpg | Snow crab legs in an orange sauce with glass noodles, in a multicoloured floral bowl, Yong Fu, Shanghai
Made with a giant snow crab (松叶蟹), served with 茭白, water bamboo, whose crisp white stems are a prized autumn speciality of Jiangnan, pickled sour in this version. The sauce is made from the pickled water bamboo with a stock of crab shells, crab roe and fish bones, and glass noodles go in. As it arrives, the aroma of crab and the sea rises to the nose, yet in the mouth there is no fishiness at all. A smooth, mellow bisque-like sauce, soft with umami from the sea and carrot, with a light creamy sweetness set against the sourness of the pickled shoots.

### Crispy Pigeon Leg Stuffed with Crab (金酥锁蟹鲜) 🕶️
image: ./images/yong-fu/12.jpg | Crispy fried pigeon leg stuffed with crab, its end tied with white string and pine needles, beside shredded bamboo shoots, Yong Fu, Shanghai
The chef debones a pigeon leg and uses its skin to wrap crab meat and crab fat, then fries it and serves it with bamboo shoots. The Chinese name means "golden crispness that locks in the freshness of crab". The pigeon skin is beautifully crisp and the filling incredibly juicy: bite in and the crab juices burst out. Rich crab roe, sweet crab and the deep richness of the pigeon, with the fat of the bird and the crab roe blending perfectly. The leg meat is bouncy and tender, the crab generous. Utterly delicious. I think this new dish could easily become the restaurant's icon.

### Red-Braised Fish Maw with Single-Clove Garlic (红焖独蒜焖鳘鱼胶)
image: ./images/yong-fu/13.jpg | Thick pieces of red-braised fish maw with single-clove garlic, in a brightly patterned Japanese porcelain bowl, Yong Fu, Shanghai
The most expensive dish of the set. It uses fish maw (花胶) from 鳘鱼, the large croaker of the East China Sea, cut thick and braised in a traditional red-braise sauce with single-clove garlic, which is sweeter and less pungent than ordinary garlic, served in a Japanese porcelain dish. The fish maw is soft and thick, pleasantly sticky without being tough. The sauce carries a clear garlic aroma that whets the appetite without any lingering harsh bite, along with the nutty note of soy and a great deal of collagen. Incredibly rich.

### Three Seasonal Vegetables (时蔬三味)
image: ./images/yong-fu/14.jpg | Three small bowls of vegetables: fox nuts, Shanghai bok choy in a white broth, and bean sprouts with heads and tails removed, Yong Fu, Shanghai
Seasonal vegetables three ways. The first is fox nut (鸡头米), the seed of an aquatic plant of the water-lily family and a prized autumn speciality of Suzhou, paired with sweet peas from Jiangsu. The second is Shanghai bok choy, using only the heart, served in a creamy white broth. The third is 银芽, silver sprouts: bean sprouts with heads and tails picked off, leaving only the white stems, with fine green threads woven through. A rare vegetable, and something distinctive I had never tasted before.

### Baked Rice with Cured Meat (腊味冬米饭)
image: ./images/yong-fu/15.jpg | Dark brown baked rice with cured meat, broad beans and Chinese kale, on a blue-and-white porcelain plate, Yong Fu, Shanghai
Made with 冬米, winter-harvested rice whose dense grains soak up liquid well, baked with finely diced cured meat, broad beans and Chinese kale. The rice is dark from the soy, plump and chewy, with a clear rice aroma, the fragrance of cured meat, and a gentle saltiness from the dried meat and soy.

### Fried Black Sesame Tangyuan (油塌汤圆)
image: ./images/yong-fu/16.jpg | Fried black sesame glutinous rice balls pressed into golden, crisp rounds, on a blue-and-white plate with a wavy rim, Yong Fu, Shanghai
The filling is 黑洋酥: roasted black sesame ground with lard and sugar. Ningbo is the original home of China's lard tangyuan (猪油汤圆), made both boiled and fried; here they are fried. The glutinous rice skin is crisp with a real crunch, soft and chewy without feeling heavy. The filling inside is scorching hot, a lava of black sesame, so take care. Sweet, fragrant with black sesame and brown sugar, and so good it tempts you to ask for another piece even when you're already full.

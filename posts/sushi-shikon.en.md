Sukiyabashi Jiro, Masa, The Araki, Sushi Yoshitake and Sushi Saito are all famous names, well known among gourmets as celebrated sushi restaurants whose booking queues run from three months to a full year. These are restaurants where even the Prime Minister of Japan or the President of the United States must book in advance, restaurants whose merits this article could never list in full. Yet there is one that people always forget to mention: "Sushi Shikon".

As these thoughts went through my mind, the subway pulled into Sheung Wan station. I tapped my Octopus card and stepped off with my partner in crime, the friend who had tricked me into this trip, heading towards Victoria Peak. After a short walk I arrived at the Mercer hotel, home of Sushi Shikon.

Sushi Shikon is one of six sushi restaurants, together with the names mentioned above, to have earned the prestigious Michelin three stars (2017)!!! When Shikon opened in 2013 it received only two Michelin stars, then moved up to three the following year and has kept all three to this day. Of those six sushi restaurants, for Thais Sushi Shikon is probably the easiest to get into, with a booking queue so short it is shocking. This may be because it is rather pale next to the other names at the same level, and because the price is sky-high.

Sushi Shikon is the one and only offspring of Sushi Yoshitake, the famous sushi restaurant in Tokyo. In its first year it still carried the name Yoshitake, like the main branch, but for some reason Shikon changed its name the following year.

Sushi Shikon is run by Chef Yoshiharu Makimura, who was for a long time the sous chef of Chef Masahiro Yoshitake before stepping up to take care of Sushi Shikon. It is said that Chef Yoshitake was so fond of Chef Yoshiharu that he gave up his one and only day off to pass his skills on to him.

Sushi Shikon is one of the most expensive restaurants in the world, in the top 10 of three-Michelin-star restaurants worldwide, with a dinner menu at 3,500 HKD (around 15,000 baht). At that price I wondered how the quality would compare with other top sushi restaurants in Japan, but a review by the well-known reviewer Andy Hayler left me with no hesitation about booking immediately. In that review Andy claims that Shikon not only has fish flown in from Japan day by day, as any good sushi restaurant does, but has fish sent from Japan twice a day to achieve the finest possible quality. Beyond ingredients such as fish, even the rice, water and charcoal are imported from Japan, so as to match the quality of the mother restaurant in Japan.

Personally, this is the highest-quality sushi restaurant I have ever eaten at, and it truly stands apart, as befits a three-star restaurant.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Kimedai Appetizer
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/01.jpg | Lightly fried kimedai with Kyoto spring onion and grated daikon in a porcelain bowl, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
Our appetizer today began with kimedai, lightly fried and eaten with Kyoto spring onion, grated daikon and dashi sauce, sprinkled with a little Japanese chilli powder. Frying the kimedai lightly made the flesh firm while keeping its sweetness very well. The right amount of chilli powder brings out the sweetness of the dish with a surprising gentleness. It is one of the best kimedai I have ever eaten.

### Flounder with Sea Cucumber
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/02.jpg | Flounder soaked in soda water with yuzu zest and pickled sea cucumber on a red-patterned plate, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The next dish was flounder soaked in soda water, eaten with yuzu zest, salt-pickled sea cucumber and wasabi. Soaking the fish in soda gives the flesh an interesting texture. The pairing of the pickled sea cucumber, slightly salty, with the sweet, pungent wasabi and the fresh fragrance of citrus zest was spot on and very well balanced.

### Flounder Fin
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/03.jpg | Flounder fin served as nigiri on a dark orange ceramic plate, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The next dish was still flounder, but here the chef chose the fin, crunchy and firm, as one piece, and the regular flesh as the other, so that we could perceive the difference. The fish was superbly fresh, even compared with famous restaurants in Japan, rich and sweet. Paired with a soy sauce blended with dashi made from the fish's innards, boiled in-house, the saltiness was gentler than ordinary soy sauce and brought out the sweetness of the fish very well.

### Sardine Roll
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/04.jpg | Sardine sashimi roll with pickled ginger and spring onion on a ceramic plate, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
A sardine sashimi roll. For this dish the chef rolled the fish with pickled ginger, pickled vegetables and spring onion, and the result is a work of art. Every proportion was spot on: the pungency of the spring onion, the fiery heat of the pickled ginger, the sweet-sour of the pickles, all meeting the fresh, rich flesh of the sardine, with the seaweed still crisp cutting across the tip of the tongue. Excellent.

### Boiled Octopus
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/05.jpg | Boiled octopus tentacle, Yoshitake style, on a ceramic plate, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The next dish is the restaurant's famous signature, inherited from Yoshitake to Shikon: boiled octopus tentacle. The restaurant massages the tentacle for a long time, then soaks it in the house sauce, which is slightly sweet. The result, in my opinion, is the best octopus tentacle I have ever eaten: it melts in the mouth yet keeps a crisp, springy texture, unlike many places where the tenderness turns mushy.

### Seared Katsuo
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/06.jpg | Seared katsuo served with sea urchin and Hokkaido grated daikon, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
Seared katsuo, eaten with a sauce made from katsuo broth, spring onion, and grated daikon from Hokkaido, with grated daikon and egg yolk. It is the best seared katsuo I have ever tried. The skin was crisp and fragrant, the doneness even throughout the whole piece. The sauce was mild and brought out the fish well, and the sweet daikon complemented the bonito flavour very well.

### Awabi
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/07.jpg | Sliced awabi (abalone) with green abalone liver sauce and wasabi, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The awabi here was the smoothest and most tender I have ever eaten. The abalone liver sauce differs from other places: it is purely rich and sweet, with none of the slight fishiness and offal smell found elsewhere.

### Abalone Liver Rice
Rice mixed with abalone liver. This was the dish I liked least in the meal. Shikon's rice is better suited to making sushi, and its acidity was too assertive for such a superb abalone liver sauce, covering the richness and sweetness of the sauce.

### Refreshment: Junsai
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/09.jpg | Junsai, or water shield shoots, with toasted rice and sea urchin in a clear glass, garnished with a slice of lime, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The restaurant's refreshment was junsai, or water shield shoots, with toasted rice soaked in sauce, uni and a slice of lime. Eating it, you begin with the crisp, sweet coolness of the shoots, then meet the saltiness of the toasted rice, then the sweetness of the uni, and finish with the sourness of the lime. A very good dish.

### Ika (Squid)
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/10.jpg | Ika (squid) sushi with translucent white squid over rice, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The first piece was squid. The squid here is softer and smoother than at ordinary restaurants, though not because it lacks freshness. The flesh had superb sweetness, with a little citrus zest sprinkled on top, but personally I feel squid should be crunchier.

### Kasugodai
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/11.jpg | Kasugodai (young snapper) sushi with silver skin over rice, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The next piece was kasugodai, young snapper. The flesh was rich and crisper than ordinary snapper, and sweet as well.

### Akami
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/12.jpg | Akami sushi, deep red tuna, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The akami here is soaked briefly in soy sauce to bring out the flavour of the fish. The flesh was very smooth, soft and moist on the tongue.

### Otoro
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/13.jpg | Otoro sushi, orange-pink tuna belly, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The otoro here is not very fatty, but what sets it apart is its softness as you chew, and the slightly sour sushi rice really brings out the flavour of the fish.

### Aji
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/14.jpg | Aji sushi, silver-faced horse mackerel topped with green wasabi, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
Aji. The aji here is of superb quality. It does not need the pungent vegetables that ordinary restaurants add: you can enjoy the sweet, rich flesh of the aji without any fishiness.

### Akagai
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/15.jpg | Akagai (ark shell) sushi over rice, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The akagai here was very fresh and smoother and softer than any I have eaten elsewhere. It is usually crunchier, but personally I found it slightly fishy.

### Uni
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/16.jpg | Uni gunkan wrapped in seaweed and topped with orange sea urchin, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The uni here is made into a gunkan with bafun and murasaki. The seaweed stays crisp, and the fresh uni, top grade, is added without restraint. Another superb dish.

### Kuruma Ebi
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/17.jpg | Kuruma ebi sushi, a beautifully red boiled prawn over rice, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The kuruma ebi here is the best of any restaurant I have eaten at. The flesh was still a lovely red, with a slight crispness of raw prawn and superb sweetness. For this piece the chef uses prawn fat, seasoned sweet and free of fishiness, to give a small pungent kick in place of wasabi, which brings out the prawn very well. Unlike some places where the prawn fat is so prominent that it covers the flavour of the flesh entirely.

### Anago
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/18.jpg | Anago sushi, grilled sea eel glazed with sauce, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The anago here is superb, melting in the mouth. The flesh is smooth and soft without losing its shape, and the fattiness of the fish remains in full. Very impressive.

### Tamago
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/19.jpg | Tamago, a golden-yellow block of sweet omelette, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
Sweet omelette, done well, to standard. The tamago here leans salty first and sweet after, with a beautifully smooth texture. It is first-rate, but I still prefer the tamago of other restaurants.

### Miso Soup
image: ./images/sushi-shikon/20.jpg | Miso soup in a red-lacquered bowl to close the meal, Sushi Shikon, Hong Kong
The miso soup here has no ingredients, just dashi and miso, but it is delicious. A little citrus zest added to it brings freshness, and combined with the superb miso it is one of the best miso soups I have ever eaten.

Kanda is a small kaiseki restaurant in Roppongi, Tokyo's famous district of restaurants and nightlife. It seats just eight guests a day, to make sure every one of them receives the best possible experience.

Kanda is one of the most controversial restaurants in Japan's fine dining circles. Well-known reviewers and bloggers (including missneverfull, a reviewer for 50 Best) have called it overrated and unworthy of its standing, and its reviews on Tabelog, the famous Japanese review site, are not especially high either. Even so, Kanda has held three Michelin stars ever since the Michelin Guide arrived in Japan, which made me think the place must have something going for it. I was lucky that the restaurant had a table free when I went, while others of the same calibre on my list, such as Ryugin and Quintessence, were fully booked, so the choice was not a hard one.

My party and I arrived slightly early, even though we had allowed plenty of time, because the restaurant is very hard to find. It sits on the ground floor of a small building in Roppongi with no landmark but a small sign reading "kanda". At first I took the building for an apartment block rather than a restaurant.

Once inside, the staff welcomed us impeccably and took our coats and belongings to a coatroom. On the wall hang framed pages bearing the signatures of the first Michelin Guide inspectors in Japan, more than ten years ago, a fine testament to the restaurant's standards. We were then shown to wait at the counter. The attention to detail begins right here: the tableware of every shape and kind lined up behind the counter, and even the handmade pottery chopstick rests.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Oyster and Fava Beans
image: ./images/kanda/01.jpg | Battered fried oyster with fava beans on a blue-and-white patterned plate, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
The first dish looks utterly simple: fried oyster with fava beans. The large oyster was the best I have ever eaten. Fresh from a remote island off Hiroshima, it was fried with perfect judgement, not raw yet still juicy, the flesh not mushy and with no fishy smell, seasoned with only a little salt to bring out its flavour. Eaten together with the fava beans, fried until completely dry, salty and rich, the pairing lifted the oyster's flavour and texture just right. Don't let the beautiful plating fool you: this dish shows the restaurant's philosophy of simplicity and balance on the plate from the very start.

### Tai and Cod
image: ./images/kanda/02.jpg | Tai and cod wrapped in mashed turnip, in a hot flower-patterned bowl, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
Tai and cod wrapped in mashed turnip, with a yuzu dashi sauce. As you eat, the slightly sweet, slightly pungent mashed turnip slowly gives way until you reach the pieces of cod and tai wrapped inside. The sweet, rich flesh of the tai and cod is brushed by the faint fragrance of yuzu and the rounded flavour of a dashi made from dried fish. It is a dish blended with exactly the right balance. Most restaurants would use taro or daikon, but having tried Kanda's version I understood why the chef chose turnip: mashed and boiled, taro turns sticky and would smother the fish, while daikon is too bland, or gets swallowed by the dashi when simmered.

### Toro Sashimi
image: ./images/kanda/03.jpg | Toro sashimi in a square green-and-gold bowl, with a small dish of salt beside it, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
Next came toro sashimi, an ordinary-looking dish with one unusual twist: it is eaten with salt. The chef said this lets the flavour of the toro show more clearly than with the usual soy sauce, though it also means that if the fish were not truly good, any fishy smell or unpleasant taste would give it away. The fish is cut into single bites with the wasabi already placed on top. In the mouth, the sweet, cool pungency of wasabi grated fresh just before serving comes first, followed by the gentle saltiness of the salt, and finally the sweetness and richness of the toro. This dish again shows god-tier skill: it looks like nothing much, yet everything is exactly right and in balance.

### Kawahagi Sashimi
image: ./images/kanda/04.jpg | Thin slices of kawahagi sashimi on a pale blue ceramic plate, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
The next dish is sashimi again, but the degree of strangeness goes up: kawahagi (filefish) wrapped around its own liver, eaten with a sauce of chilli and pickled cherry. I would not say I have never met a dish like this anywhere. The richness and crispness of the flesh and liver, prepared to fit a single bite, are tamed by the characteristically sharp, spicy sauce, which cuts the intensity of the kawahagi very well. The sauce and the fish seem to contradict each other, yet they come together in a strangely perfect way. You could call it an amusement park for the taste buds.

### Poached Prawn Dumplings
image: ./images/kanda/05.jpg | Clear soup with prawn dumplings, bamboo shoots and yuzu peel in a black lacquer bowl, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
This bowl is poached prawn dumplings with bamboo shoots, komatsuna and yuzu peel. Personally I found it too bland, but the cooking was flawless. The bamboo shoots had none of the usual unpleasant smell, only a faint fragrance of their own, and the komatsuna kept its fresh, sweet crunch even after simmering. Both vegetables are sweet. The dumplings are made from chopped prawn that is hardly seasoned at all, so that you taste the full sweetness of the prawn itself, and the yuzu peel lends a delicate scent. It is a warm, easy-to-eat dish that genuinely makes the heart swell.

### Sushi Rice with Pufferfish Air Bladder
image: ./images/kanda/06.jpg | Sushi rice sprinkled with grilled seaweed and salt on a blue ceramic plate, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
Sushi rice with seared pufferfish air bladder, seasoned with grilled seaweed and salt. The aroma of the charred air bladder, smooth and soft like a custard with the taste of rich milk and no fishy smell, is balanced by the warmth and slight sourness of the sushi rice. Both are lifted further by the umami of the fragrant grilled seaweed and the saltiness of the salt.

### Sawara Marinated in Miso
image: ./images/kanda/07.jpg | Fried miso-marinated sawara on a blue-and-white plate with fried lotus root, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
Sawara (Spanish mackerel) marinated in miso and fried, eaten with fried Brussels sprouts. Finding Brussels sprouts in a restaurant like this surprised me, and what is more they were prepared by pulling the leaves apart and frying them until slightly crisp, which turned out wonderful. They bring a distinctive bitterness and the aroma of Brussels sprouts, in contrast with the flesh of the sawara, which stays rich and sweet. The miso marinade is intense, but not too much, and it brings out the flavour of the fish beautifully. This dish may look ordinary, but a great deal of thought must have gone into it before it reached the plate.

### Miyazaki Wagyu
image: ./images/kanda/08.jpg | Perfectly cooked Miyazaki wagyu beef with young greens and seaweed, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
Fried Miyazaki wagyu with watercress, one of the restaurant's signature dishes. The Miyazaki beef used here is not heavily marbled, and it is fried in a thin coating of flour until it makes you think of a steak. It is cooked with precision, so the meat stays juicy. It is seasoned with just a little salt, cut with the pungency of wasabi, or with the watercress salad.

### Toast with Monkfish Liver
image: ./images/kanda/09.jpg | Toast with monkfish liver on a jade-green ceramic tray, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
Toast with monkfish liver (ankimo). The toast smells of bread toasted over charcoal in the restaurant, with an evenly crisp surface across the whole slice, soaked in butter and a sweet, rich sauce. What surprised me is that the surface of the toast stays crisp despite the butter and the sauce. That crispness contrasts with the soft, rich ankimo, true to its name as the foie gras of the sea, without the slightest fishiness. The flavour is deep and delicious, and I thought it French rather than Japanese. The dish was probably inspired by the time Mr Kanda spent working in Paris.

### Beef Tongue with Taro
image: ./images/kanda/10.jpg | Beef tongue and taro in clear broth in a red-and-gold bowl, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
Beef tongue simmered with taro. The tongue is braised until it melts, then simmered again with the taro, which reduces the cloying richness of beef tongue that many people, myself included, dislike. The sweet, rich taro, boiled until soft but not mushy, draws out the aroma and flavour of the tongue very well. Yuzu is once again the key player that stops this dish from becoming too heavy. It may look like a rich, heavy dish, yet you can keep eating it comfortably.

### Hairy Crab Rice in Hoba Leaf
image: ./images/kanda/11.jpg | Hairy crab rice wrapped in a hoba leaf on a large blue-and-white plate, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
Hairy crab rice in a hoba leaf, another of my favourite dishes of the meal. When the leaf is opened, the cool fragrance of the hoba leaf and the sweet aroma of the hairy crab drift up to the nose. The sweetness of the crab meat suits the lightly salted seasoned rice, and together they become the umami of this dish.

### Warabimochi
image: ./images/kanda/12.jpg | Warabimochi dusted with roasted soybean flour on a dark brown plate, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
The first dessert was warabimochi, which I thought was made to a decent standard, but from a restaurant of this calibre I still expected something more wow.

### Salted Caramel Ice Cream
image: ./images/kanda/13.jpg | Salted caramel ice cream in a lidded cup on a dotted saucer, Nihonryori Kanda, Tokyo
The second dessert was salted caramel ice cream drizzled with kokuto syrup. This one was made just to my liking. The ice cream is smooth and soft, as expected, with a leaning towards saltiness and the aroma of caramel. Together with the kokuto syrup it gives just the right sweetness, a complete close to the meal.

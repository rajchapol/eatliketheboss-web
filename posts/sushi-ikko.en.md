Sushi Ikko was one of the places I was most looking forward to on this trip, because it is one of the hardest restaurants to book in Japan. It holds a Tabelog Gold award, shared by only 30 restaurants across Japan, with a score of 4.53, and it also holds two Michelin stars.

Ikko is a restaurant that is less famous than it deserves to be, perhaps because of its location in a far-flung city like Hokkaido. Compared with restaurants of the same level such as Amamoto, Kimura, Mitani, Saito, Sugita and Tenzuru Kyomachi, Ikko may look much easier to book, though in truth it is still hard. It is an easy choice for anyone who wants to eat at a Tabelog Gold (2019) sushi restaurant.

For me, Sushi Ikko was very easy to reach: just a few minutes from my hotel in the Tanuki Koji area. It is one of the high-end restaurants on the island of Hokkaido that is easy for tourists to get to.

Sushi Ikko is on the second floor of a small commercial building. The sign downstairs has no English and the restaurant is rather hard to find, so I recommend arriving a little early to give yourself time to locate it.

Sushi Ikko, or Ikkou, has just one counter and opens only two sittings a day, with seven guests per sitting. That is even fewer than Miyakawa, the other famous sushi restaurant in the city. As for the decor, Ikko's counter is made of three large pieces of hinoki joined together, all chosen by Chef Kudo himself. When I saw this counter on walking in, I was a little stunned. It is simply beautiful.

Junya Kudo is a genius sushi chef with sushi in his blood. He followed in the footsteps of his grandfather and father as a sushi chef and began training from a young age, starting to shape sushi at 18, before taking over Sushi Ikko from his father at only 25. He rose quickly to two Michelin stars and won Tabelog Gold this past year. He is a fiery young sushi chef whose fame has grown extremely fast.

Chef Kudo has a passion for cooking the best he can, and seeks out only the best to serve his guests. He is rather particular about ingredients, so much so that he goes out with the fishermen to the fishing grounds to make sure of the source and quality of the fish he serves. For tuna, the chef chooses fish from Oma, said to be the best tuna in the world. For uni he must use specially raised uni from a small farm in a remote village in the Kushiro area.

This makes the price of his meal fairly high compared with other restaurants in Hokkaido; it is actually one of the most expensive on this northern island. But personally I think it is one of the best-value meals, in my view. Just watching this charming young chef shape his sushi like painting a work of art is more than worth it. If I have the chance to come back to Hokkaido, I certainly would not miss visiting Ikko again.

Junya Kudo's shaping is like art, with charm to spare. Apart from the occasional sound of cooking, almost every guest in the restaurant seemed spellbound by the beauty of the soft, flowing movements of the young chef with a smile on his face. His knife work is gentle and meticulous, like a surgeon's incision, so much so that a foreign food critic has said that Kudo-san's shaping is more like ballet. Personally, though, I felt that the shaping was not yet consistent, with a few pieces slightly loose. But he has a very long future ahead, considering how young he is for a sushi chef.

His rice is firm and crisp without being dry, with very little acidity, a strong fragrance of rice and a hint of salt. I think it suits his style of cooking, which puts great emphasis on showing off the magnificent quality of superb ingredients.

As for service, Chef Kudo himself can communicate only in limited English, but the waitress speaks excellent English, so a foreign guest like me did not struggle much. The chef tells the story of the ingredients and how they are prepared while shaping gently with his hands, then explains to the guests in Japanese before serving. So for everything the chef said I had to ask, and the waitress would translate. Sometimes I felt too shy to ask about every piece, so some of the enjoyment may have been lost, but if you are curious about anything in particular you can ask.

The restaurant also serves cold water, just like Sushi Miyakawa, probably because of the bitter cold of this city. The drinks menu is only in Japanese, but our waitress gave excellent recommendations throughout the meal. The sake selection is rather limited, mostly sake from producers in the Hokkaido region, unlike Miyakawa, which has rare sake from top producers all over Japan, some of which are sold only to certain restaurants. Have a look at the photos to see what was on the menu this time.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Kue
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/01.jpg | Thinly sliced kue sashimi beside wasabi and salt on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
The first dish was kue sashimi. The chef soaks it in kombu dashi before serving. It had a little sinew, was very crisp and slightly rich, and was barely seasoned at all, putting the emphasis on showing off the superb ingredient.

### Awabi
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/02.jpg | Sliced awabi topped with dark liver on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
The second dish was awabi. It is one of the most distinctive abalone I have ever eaten. It was served without any sauce at all, the flesh half raw, half cooked yet very soft, and with no fishiness at all even without sauce. The first bite was slightly sweet and salty, while the second was eaten with the liver, which was more bitter, truly bitter, but did not catch in the throat. It was a grown-up kind of flavour.

### Maguro
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/03.jpg | Tuna sushi wrapped in grilled nori with seaweed sauce on an embossed glass plate, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
Maguro was next. The chef grills the seaweed until crisp, wraps it around the fish and pours a sauce made from seaweed over it. This was divine. The crisp seaweed, with its slightly burnt fragrance, went wonderfully with the sweetness of the akami, and the sauce had umami from the seaweed and also covered the bitterness of the burnt seaweed. Very impressive.

### Shirako
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/04.jpg | Creamy white shirako in an embossed glass bowl, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
Then the shirako. The chef chooses shirako from deep-sea fish caught in the deepest sea in Japan, which he says is purer. I have to say this one was truly outstanding. It was served almost unseasoned, with just a little dashi and a thin sudachi broth. The shirako still had a slightly springy texture and did not dissolve into softness, and the flesh was delicately sweet with a faint coconut aroma, very distinctive. To call this dish superb and perfect would not be an exaggeration. I have eaten good shirako at many leading restaurants in Japan, but nowhere like this.

### Ankimo
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/05.jpg | Sliced steamed ankimo on a brown ceramic plate, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
The next piece was ankimo, steamed and dressed with a lightly sweet sauce. It was gently sweet and served warm, with a little texture, not mushy, and a faint fragrance of ginger. Done to the standard of the restaurant.

### Kasugo
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/06.jpg | Kasugo sushi on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
The chef used very good fish for this kasugo, firm and rich. The fish was made to stand out by rice that was barely seasoned, finished with a fragrant, sweet wasabi with heat at the tip of the tongue. Very good.

### Sayori
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/07.jpg | Shiny silver-skinned sayori sushi on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
Sayori. The chef used top-quality fish as usual, firm and very crisp, but the wasabi here was rather too hot and fiery, covering some of the sayori's deliciousness. Ikko's pickled ginger is sharply sour, which I did not much care for, though I can understand it, since the chef's rice is hardly sour at all.

### Kinmedai
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/08.jpg | Lightly grilled kinmedai sushi on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
The next piece, kinmedai, was superb too. Firm flesh with a fragrant smokiness, blending in harmony with the fiery wasabi.

### Akami
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/09.jpg | Deep red akami sushi from Oma tuna, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
The akami here uses fish from Oma. This piece is one of my favourite sushi, and I have to say I have never met an akami anywhere with such sweetness. The firm, sweet flesh set against the fiery wasabi was superb.

### O toro
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/10.jpg | Glossy pale pink otoro sushi on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
Now the otoro. The chef cuts it in an unusual way, with a band of sinew in the middle. Some people may like it, but personally I did not. Overall this piece was very sweet because, as always, it uses tuna from Oma, but the richness gushed out considerably more than in the previous piece. Delicious.

### Uni
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/11.jpg | Uni gunkan wrapped in seaweed and topped with orange uni on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
Next was uni gunkan. The chef used rather a lot of rice in this one, and at first I worried it would cover the uni, but it actually made the uni stand out nicely. It was a very delicious uni gunkan. The fragrant, crisp seaweed went well with the uni, and the top-quality uni itself was very delicious and sweet. It is a superb product that I personally like even better than Hakodate, which had long been number one in my heart.

### Aji
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/12.jpg | Aji sushi topped with ginger on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
Aji. For this fish the chef said timing is critical: too young is no good, and too mature is not delicious. The fish was very crisp and firm, with a faint fragrance of ginger, showing superb quality of ingredient. It is done to a high standard, but I was still more impressed by Miyakawa-san's aji, which I ate just a few days before.

### Akagai
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/13.jpg | Orange-red akagai sushi on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
On to shellfish with akagai. Before serving, the chef cuts the clam with a knife, using a balled-up white cloth beneath it as he cuts. The chef said that doing it this way gives a better texture. This piece was crisper and bouncier than many akagai I have eaten, with a faint sudachi fragrance and no bloody smell. Done very well.

### Buri
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/14.jpg | Glossy pink buri sushi on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
Now one of the chef's signature dishes, buri. He cuts it with a special technique that makes the fish extremely soft. This piece was very good. The buri was soft and rich like fine otoro, truly soft, so good that I could not believe buri sushi could taste this good. Everyone around the counter that day let out a cry of surprise.

### Anago
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/15.jpg | Warm grilled anago sushi sprinkled with salt on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
The closing piece was anago, sea eel, served warm. The chef did not brush it with sauce but sprinkled it with salt, which surprised me greatly, but once I ate it, it showed the formidable technique and the brilliance of Kudo Junya. Although the fish had no sauce, it was intensely sweet within, in perfect contrast with the saltiness of the salt on top. Very delicious. From the very start of the meal I had noticed that the chef had two sauce containers, both of soy sauce, one rather bland and the other slightly sweet, and I wondered what he would do with the eel. By this point the question in my mind had been answered.

### Sumi Ika
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/16.jpg | Pale white sumi ika sushi on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
An additional piece was sumi ika. The restaurant used a superb squid; although the cut was thick, it was very crisp and very firm, with a faint fragrance of green sudachi. The chef barely seasoned this piece at all, letting the quality of the ingredient tell its own story.

### Tuna Maki
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/17.jpg | Three tekka maki pieces wrapped in seaweed with tuna filling on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
Another additional piece was tekka maki. This one was unusual, dressed with sauce on top. The top-grade fish still shone through, but I felt the sauce on top made the seaweed slightly chewy.

### Tamago Yaki
image: ./images/sushi-ikko/18.jpg | A single golden-yellow piece of tamago on a wooden counter, Sushi Ikko, Sapporo
The sweet omelette here smelled of egg and vanilla. The texture was fairly firm and creamy, yet soft and smooth going down.

Kashiwaya is a famous kappo kaiseki restaurant from Osaka, renowned enough to have opened a branch across the sea in Hong Kong.

Kashiwaya is highly regarded by the Michelin Guide, which has awarded it three prestigious stars for a long time. It also has a fairly high Tabelog score of 4.19, and even its Hong Kong branch has won two Michelin stars.

On the other hand, the well-known food blogger Andy Hayler (the first man in the world to have visited every three-Michelin-star restaurant) doubts it, calling the place a little overrated, which left me quite curious whether it was worth a visit.

However, because I booked and researched at very short notice, less than a week before the trip (really about four days), and during Golden Week, several other options of mine, such as Koryu, Fujiya1932 and La Cime, were either closed or fully booked. Kashiwaya managed to find me a table at short notice, for which I am very grateful.

Kashiwaya today is run by the second-generation chef-owner, Hideaki Matsuo, who has a degree in physics. He wants to bring modern Western influences into the restaurant's food under the concept of "Japanese cuisine of tomorrow". It seems rather paradoxical, then, that the chef is also devoted to ancient traditions such as the tea ceremony and other old Japanese cultures, which made me all the more curious what the food at Kashiwaya would be like.

At Kashiwaya every table dines in a private room. The most striking, though I had no chance to visit it, is apparently the room for large parties, a piece of joinery by a famous Japanese artist. A room for two, however, uses the tea ceremony room for serving.

At the dinner I attended, the food at Kashiwaya starts at 16,000 yen and goes up to 40,000 yen, while lunch starts at 10,000 yen, excluding a 10% service charge and 8% tax. If you can, and have time at midday, lunch is far better value. The restaurant's location is rather far from the tourist districts, though, which may make it a little awkward to fit into your itinerary.

The sake pairing costs 11,000 yen, which may seem expensive, but compared with the sake served it is very good value. Many of the sakes are not easy to find and are sold only to selected restaurants. What is truly excellent is the carefully thought-out sake pairing, which suited every dish very well.

That evening I took the Midosuji subway line from Shinsaibashi to Esaka, then a taxi to Kashiwaya at Senriyama. For some unavoidable reason I had to phone the restaurant to postpone my booking, and I arrived rather late, but a member of staff was already standing at the front waiting to welcome us as soon as we stepped out of the taxi. That impressed me greatly.

Service at Kashiwaya is almost perfect. The two waitresses who looked after me were attentive to detail, with a very high standard of service: knocking at the door every time before serving, wiping up any spill every time, and small thoughtful touches, such as suggesting I leave my souvenir without its box because I would need to pack for the flight the next day, which would be more convenient. They tried to communicate in English and explained the details of the dishes, and although one of the waitresses was over forty, her nuggets of knowledge about Japanese culture and the care she showed over each plate demonstrated the highest level of professionalism.

I had reserved in advance the special set celebrating the change of era to Reiwa, together with the sake pairing. Let's see from the photos what we had.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Hamo
image: ./images/kashiwaya/01.jpg | Hamo arranged on a square plate, garnished with green leaves, chopsticks in front, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
The first dish served was hamo, or pike eel, lightly seasoned, with kinome, koshiabura and myoga. Hamo is very hard to cook well, and Kashiwaya's hamo is prepared to an excellent standard, with no sense of bones left at all. It has sweetness in the flesh and a chewy skin. The dish is only lightly seasoned so that we taste the full sweetness of the fish, lifted by pungent vegetables and a little vinegar. It was paired with Meikyoshisui nama junmai ginjo, served chilled, fairly sweet, with an apple aroma, which brought out the hamo well.

### Ise Ebi Soup
image: ./images/kashiwaya/02.jpg | Clear soup in a black lacquer bowl with green tofu and pumpkin, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
The next dish was a slow-cooked ise ebi soup with pumpkin, mung bean tofu and yuzu peel. It was fairly sweet, the prawn flesh rather soft, melting and sweet, with no fishy smell at all. I don't know whether that was intentional, because personally I prefer prawn that is crisp and springy, but Kashiwaya's technique for cooking prawn is distinctive, and it goes very well with a soup that is salty, aromatic and sweet at the tip of the tongue. What amazed me in this dish was the chewy tofu mixed with beans, which makes the sweetness of the prawn stand out strongly. Truly impressive. Here we ate with Hououbiden junmai ginjo, served hot, so it blended seamlessly with the soup. This glass of sake is mild and soft, aromatic, with a refreshing aftertaste, which lifts the sweetness and goes very well with the ise ebi.

### Sashimi
image: ./images/kashiwaya/03.jpg | Seasonal sashimi in a flower-patterned bowl, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
Our sashimi was seasonal fish: tai, sweet prawn and seared katsuo, with young shiso shoots and wasabi. It came with the restaurant's special soy sauce blended with kombu, and with ponzu. Our waitress recommended eating the katsuo with ponzu and the rest with soy sauce. The sashimi is at the standard of a good restaurant, but what clearly stands out is the restaurant's soy sauce, made specially for it entirely from natural ingredients, kombu and bonito, with a mellow, gentle flavour and none of the sweetness that clings to the tongue. We ate it with Kokuryu 38 junmai daiginjo, very pure and smooth, which went well with the sashimi.

### Unryuyaki
image: ./images/kashiwaya/04.jpg | Golden souffle in a blue-and-white bowl on a blue leaf-shaped plate, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
The next dish is the restaurant's signature: its hairy crab souffle, called Unryuyaki. The restaurant uses pine nuts and sweet potato instead of butter, giving it a Japanese character. This dish is outstanding, impressive and very distinctive. The souffle is soft, light and airy, piping hot and fragrant with richness, mirin and a faint nuttiness, which suits the full sweetness of the hairy crab. The sugar crystals on the rim of the bowl add a touch more sweetness, perfectly. We ate it with Michizakura served cold, slightly sour, which gently lifts the sweetness of the crab meat.

### Hassun
image: ./images/kashiwaya/05.jpg | Hassun on a bow-shaped wooden tray with green vegetables and small grilled items, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
The hassun was served on a bow-shaped plate, a symbol of boys, because the next day was Boys' Day in Japan. The plate held a chilled green pea soup; chimaki (a Japanese stuffed sweet) in the restaurant's own recipe, made with sushi rice and tai; yahatamaki, or eel wrapped around burdock; stuffed anchovy with butterbur; and boiled fava beans. They may look ordinary, but every item was superbly made, especially the pea soup, deeply delicious from a carefully prepared dashi combined with the full sweetness of the peas. Another piece I want to mention is the wrapped eel, piping hot, sweet and juicy, done very well. Because this dish is quite intense in flavour, the restaurant chose to pair it with a sake of firm, sweet, concentrated character, Yukinobijin junmai ginjo. Personally I think this dish needed a somewhat lighter, fresher sake, which would have drawn out the natural flavour of the fish more interestingly.

### Yakimono: Grilled Abalone
Our yakimono was grilled abalone, piping hot and chewy, the surface crisp and crunchy with a slight charcoal aroma. The abalone liver was astonishing, with not the slightest fishiness and a deep, delicious seasoning. Astonishing and utterly delicious. For this dish the restaurant chose to pair Kokuryu junmai ginjo from Fukui Prefecture, served at 13 degrees. Its coolness, melon aroma and soft sweetness brought out the abalone's own character very well.

### Tairagai 🕶️
image: ./images/kashiwaya/07.jpg | Asparagus and shellfish on a yellow sauce in a pale blue dish, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
The next dish was another of my favourites of the meal. The restaurant served tairagai, slowly cooked and then aburi-seared, alongside two-colour asparagus and an egg yolk sauce. Pairing shellfish and asparagus, both ingredients of delicate sweetness, with an egg yolk sauce of such intensity made me doubt they would suit each other, but the restaurant proved me wrong. The lightly seasoned yolk sauce, deep in flavour, lifted the sweetness of the shellfish and asparagus just right. The asparagus was sweet, and the tairagai tender, sweet, crisp and faintly smoky, and the dish was finished perfectly with a thin slice of sudachi lemon to cleanse the mouth. Utterly delicious. The restaurant served this dish with Hokujushi junmai ginjo, cold. Personally I felt this dish did not quite suit the sake, because the yolk in the mouth overwhelmed the sake's aroma.

### Hachimono
image: ./images/kashiwaya/08.jpg | Fish and green tofu skin in a tall white bowl, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
Our hachimono was sea eel, eaten with tofu skin mixed with yomogi and shaped by hand, topped with finely sliced sweet beans, and served with a warm sauce made from dashi and kudzu powder produced in the Yoshino district of Nara. The restaurant did this dish very well. The sauce is umami but not so heavy that it covers the sweetness of the ingredients. The sweet beans on top add crunch as you chew, and the grated ginger lifts the sweetness and adds freshness to the dish. It may look simple, but it is truly delicious. Because this dish is fairly intense, the restaurant paired it with an intense sake, Fukuchitose junmai daiginjo, whose long, sweet aftertaste was brought out clearly by the spicy grated ginger. It is remarkable that the grated ginger not only lifts the sweetness of everything on the plate but also lifts the drink.

### Rice
image: ./images/kashiwaya/09.jpg | Baked rice in a clay pot topped with green and orange vegetables, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
Then came the rice course, for which Kashiwaya cooks rice for the guests in an old-style rice pot. Kashiwaya's rice dish was baked rice with scallops, karasumi (a kind of dried fish roe) and mitsuba. It was very delicious, and there was a great deal of it (haha). The scallops were sweet and intense, and the saltiness and distinctive crunch of the karasumi went with the piping hot rice, freshened by the mitsuba sprinkled on top, before finishing with a silky seaweed soup. It was so good that I had to ask them to pack the rest to take home. This dish came with Kirinzan junmai ginjo, served warm, which is very dry and suits baked rice.

### Honey Jelly
image: ./images/kashiwaya/10.jpg | Fruit jelly in a clear glass on a metal tray with a small tomato on top, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
Honey jelly was our first dessert. What impressed me about the restaurant's jelly was the aroma of honey, a sweetness that was not excessive, and how smoothly it went down. The sweetness of the honey goes well with the sour fruits included, kiwi, orange, raspberry, cherry and blueberry.

### Aonori Kinton
image: ./images/kashiwaya/11.jpg | Green shredded sweet on a black lacquer plate, Kashiwaya Senriyama, Osaka
The last dessert of the evening was aonori kinton, filled with red bean paste and served with matcha, paired with mirin, the Japanese sweet liquor, of the Kokonoe brand. Personally I found the distinctive aroma of the mirin a little too strong.

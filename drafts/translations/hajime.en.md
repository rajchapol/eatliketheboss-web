Without a doubt, Hajime in Osaka is one of the best French restaurants in Japan, perhaps even in the world. In price, too, it is one of the highest.

Hajime is the most expensive restaurant I have ever eaten at. At first I hesitated for a long time over whether to come, because of the painful price (you may not see me post on the blog for a while), and there was also a two-star restaurant with excellent Tabelog reviews that happened to have a seat free for me. But after asking my team and the friends among the page's fans by DM, everyone said "worth trying", "good things must be tried", "once in a lifetime", and so on. Oh well, grit my teeth and go.

What makes Hajime so special that our team even wanted me to try it? The restaurant won three Michelin stars faster than any restaurant in the history of the Michelin Guide Japan, and Chef Yoneda earned his first three stars faster than any chef in Michelin Guide history, just one year and five months after opening in 2008. In addition, Hajime has a Tabelog score of 4.27, in the top ten in Osaka; it long featured on Asia's 50 Best Restaurants before dropping off recently; and it is on OAD's list of the best restaurants in Japan: everything a chef could dream of.

Hajime Yoneda, the owner-chef, studied engineering and worked for a while before discovering himself, so he quit and flew to France to train. He then returned to Japan and worked as sous chef for a time at one of the best French restaurants in Japan, Michel Bras at Lake Toya in Hokkaido, before finally opening his own restaurant.

That evening I took the subway to Higobashi Station, not far from Shinsaibashi, the famous pedestrian shopping street. After a few minutes' walk I reached Hajime. A warning: Japanese restaurants are very serious about time. Please arrive on time, or tell the restaurant in advance if something unavoidable comes up.

The concept of Chef Yoneda's food is to present the beauty and peace of nature, the earth and space, with a fierce spirit of development and the utmost commitment to attention to detail.

Because the chef's hobby is painting, each of Chef Yoneda's dishes is extremely beautiful, like a canvas on which the beauty of nature is composed onto the plate. His plating is strongly influenced by Michel Bras, so much so that a gourmet at the next table even spoke up.

Hajime pays attention to every detail so that the food is served in its most perfect condition. Inside the restaurant it is rather warm so that our food doesn't cool too quickly; and when I asked about the toilet, the waiter warned me that the food was about to be served and asked me to wait a moment.

In the past the restaurant even forbade photography so that we would eat immediately, but today this rule seems much relaxed since the chef took up Instagram (@hajimeyoneda, go and follow him). We can take photos as long as there is no flash, no sound, and no disturbance to other guests.

The menu is a tasting menu only, at 45,360 yen, plus a short tasting menu at 33,480 yen, excluding 8% tax and a 10% service charge, which makes Hajime without doubt one of the most expensive restaurants in the world.

For me, Hajime's food is not for everyone. Personally, I think it is very beautiful, but it needs a fair amount of interpretation and understanding, like an abstract painting, because beyond the intricate flavours and the delicacy of each dish, Hajime's food has to be interpreted to find the concept of each dish and understand it deeply.

Chef Hajime Yoneda's food is not presented directly; it presents the idea behind it, through a conversation in the language of food between us and the world, by way of the chef.

Another thing that impressed me about Chef Yoneda is his determination to keep the standard consistent from start to finish. The chef comes out himself to thank guests and see off every table, a sight that is very rarely seen from a famous chef like this.

Today I chose the full tasting menu because I wanted to try Chikyu, the chef's signature dish, with the non-alcohol pairing, which was very cheeky. Let's see what there was from the photos. Since each dish has a great deal of detail, there may be omissions or mistakes, and I apologise for that here.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Midori
image: ./images/hajime/01.jpg | Green soup in a white cup on a wooden stand, Hajime, Osaka
The first dish to arrive is Midori, or green. Thinking of nature, the first thing that comes to mind is probably lush green leaves, but it is really not only that. In this dish the chef presents a white asparagus pudding with a sauce of green asparagus, eaten with chickweed and a yuzu sauce. The first bite brings the fresh aroma of asparagus from the finely smooth pudding and the warm oil sauce, followed by the sweet-and-sour of the yuzu, which is both well matched and wonderfully cuts the richness. In addition, the roasted chickweed seeds (chickweed, hakobe) add fragrance and dimension in the mouth in a fun way.

### Seimei
image: ./images/hajime/02.jpg | A thin cracker resting on a dark stone, decorated with young greens, Hajime, Osaka
The next dish is Seimei, or life. Here the chef presents the tiny living things that are the foundation of the ecosystem, through a fish cracker with watercress and herring roe. The other piece is fried eel with watercress cream and matcha pine nuts. Both pieces are served slightly warm. The fish cracker is crisp and slightly sweet. I like the lightly salty herring roe on top, which gently brings out the sweetness of the fish in the cracker. The foam and the watercress add an airy freshness and bring this bite together in a way worthy of the word life.

The other piece has a little more intensity, with the fried eel, where we sense the creaminess of the fat in the fish, before it is cut by the green freshness of various vegetables so that this bite is not too heavy. Another interesting thing in this bite is that the matcha pine nuts blend it together very well.

### Iso
image: ./images/hajime/03.jpg | Oysters on the shell set in red-purple seaweed, with cold vapour floating around the plate, Hajime, Osaka
The next course is Iso, or the rocky seashore. The chef chose to present it through three creatures that live there: oyster, mussel and sea urchin. The oyster is served with yoghurt and cucumber gel, uni, a foam from a stock of assorted sea creatures, and star anise sauce. Lastly, the mussel, quinoa and red perilla.

Personally, I like the oyster bite most. The oyster is very sweet even eaten fresh, without anything sour to bring out its flavour. The smooth, milk-scented yoghurt brings out the oyster and blends with the cucumber gel, which adds freshness to this bite in an interesting way. A very delicious bite.

The uni was just as interesting. What amazed me in this dish was the sea foam, done very well, with a complex, slightly salty flavour that brings out the sweetness of the uni superbly. The mussel seemed the least interesting bite in this dish, though not because it wasn't delicious.

### Kawa
image: ./images/hajime/04.jpg | Salmon on a long white plate decorated with dots of green sauce and various young vegetables, Hajime, Osaka
The next dish is Kawa, or river, the origin of life. What could present it better than salmon swimming upstream from the sea? The chef takes cherry salmon and cooks it slowly for 30 minutes, served with celery and mango, apple purée with dill, white beans with Hatcho miso and kaffir lime, pickled daikon, and a sauce of sorrel. Every piece in this dish is delicious, even the white beans. What is remarkable is that although every bite has a different flavour, they come together in a mellow harmony: the sweetness of the Hatcho miso, the sweet-and-sour of the apple, and the fresh taste of the many vegetables, both sorrel and celery, all help bring out the sweet, fat-rich flesh of the salmon beautifully.

Kawa is eaten with the restaurant's own ginger ale, with a distinctive spiciness from ginger, cinnamon and nutmeg.

### Chikyu 🕶️
image: ./images/hajime/05.jpg | Vegetables in a white mound on a large indigo-blue round plate, Hajime, Osaka
The next dish is Chikyu, or earth, the restaurant's signature. The photo may be deceptive, but this plate is very large; it is the size of a whole table. The chef takes more than 100 kinds of vegetables, originating from the mountains to the seashore, and prepares them in every way that brings out each vegetable's deliciousness: pickling, boiling, grilling, frying, roasting. We eat it with a seafood foam. This dish is complex and demands a great deal of labour from the chef team. It conveys the beauty of the ecosystem and of biodiversity, with the distinctive flavour of each vegetable drawn out beautifully. I recommend eating slowly, one bite at a time, and absorbing the deliciousness of nature, the diversity of flavours and the excellence of the Hajime chef team.

This glass can be called the restaurant's signature: the restaurant sun-dries tomato cores to obtain a tomato-flavoured juice that is gentler, not very intense, and without the red colour that might make you wonder whether it is really tomato juice. It is mixed with assorted herbs such as rosemary, spearmint, basil and shiso into a distinct character that cannot be imitated. The gentle, fresh taste of this drink brings out the deliciousness of the various vegetables wonderfully. This drink alone deserves appreciation, but pairing it with a dish as beautiful as Chikyu makes Chef Hajime all the more deserving of admiration for food that is like art on a plate.

### Umi: Flow
image: ./images/hajime/06.jpg | Fish on a green sauce and clear broth, on a white plate, Hajime, Osaka
Umi, or sea, is divided into two dishes. The first, Flow, is tilefish, sous-vide separately to a half-cooked, half-raw state, with the skin fried crisp, eaten with a seafood dashi mixed with kaffir lime, coriander oil, seaweed, aubergine, and daikon sprayed with lemon. Water sweeping through the stream of life is the message the chef wants to convey in this dish. The half-cooked fish is firm, and the crisp skin is a truly new sensation. Eaten with the mellow dashi mixed with refreshing coriander oil, served warm, it is even more delicious.

Potato bread and two kinds of seaweed butter. The bread is slightly sweet, and the seaweed butter, whose richness is less intense than ordinary butter, goes well with this fish.

We eat it with Gyokuro tea from Uji, Kyoto Prefecture.

### Umi: Relation
image: ./images/hajime/07.jpg | Prawn and langoustine on a round plate shaded in blues, decorated with white foam and small vegetables, Hajime, Osaka
The second Umi dish is Relation, a dish presented through the relationship of the fraternal twins prawn and langoustine. The prawn is seasoned with curry powder, while the langoustine is served with a consommé jelly, alongside apple purée, lemon purée, quinoa, raw quail egg yolk, ham ravioli and olive oil foam. This is another dish I like, for the variety put into it, in both ingredients and flavours. Although it looks complicated, everything is resolved without overpowering anything else: the sweetness of the prawn meat, the spicy heat of the curry powder, the sweetness of the langoustine and the saltiness of the consommé, the salty ham ravioli, and the refreshing sweet-and-sour fruit purées.

We eat it with a boiled vegetable broth, which I think is far too ordinary.

### Hakai to Douka
image: ./images/hajime/08.jpg | A golden crisp rolled snack on a black stone slab, Hajime, Osaka
Hakai to Douka, the dish of destruction and decay. The chef chose to present it with foie gras wrapped in sugar candy of Asia hazel. For the food of destruction, the chef chose to present two villainous flavours, sweetness and fat, and what could present them better than foie gras and candy? What is remarkable about this dish is that the foie gras is not as cloying as one would think, even though nothing sour helps to cut it, only a faint black pepper. An excellent and truly astonishing dish.

We eat it with rice tea to reduce the richness.

### Hope
image: ./images/hajime/09.jpg | Sliced Wagyu on a long white plate, dressed with beautiful green and yellow sauces, Hajime, Osaka
When there is an end, there must be a beginning: Hope. This dish is divided into three parts. The first part is a dish called dew, which helps seedlings grow again. The chef chose to present it with something I never thought anyone would do in this lifetime, a sorbet of pine leaves. The bitterness of this dish is very distinctive: it may be truly bitter, yet it feels refreshing, moistening to the throat, with a cool fragrance. A dish that may look like nothing, yet is astonishing and very delicious.

The second part of Hope is Mother Earth, roasted Wagyu from Kagoshima. A dish very full of flavour and presented very interestingly. The meat is tender and rich, lightly salted by the salt sprinkled on it, and matches the sauce and the sweet pumpkin purée perfectly, alongside parsnip and orange zest and a sweet-and-sour yellow tomato, before being cut with wasabi leaf, spicy and pungent, and a bitter matcha sauce so that it is not too rich.

This drink looks like nothing, yet it is a great deal: sparkling mineral water scented with hinoki wood, which adds freshness and fragrance and cleanses the flavours left in the mouth remarkably. At first I was rather puzzled that the chef chose to serve the beef before the duck, and what is more, Wagyu, which is rich and fatty; a very bold order of service. But after trying this glass, I rather understood the chef's intention.

The third part is sky: duck from Kyoto, smoked over cherry wood, eaten with assorted vegetables, with the distinctive, appetising intensity added by black garlic purée and a little pungency from mustard leaf purée. Personally, I thought the duck skin was a little too tough, but I can understand the chef's intention to show the chewy texture and the crisp surface of duck skin.

Koshu grape juice: Koshu is a famous Japanese grape that is often made into wine. The grape juice the restaurant uses is the same kind used to make wine, unfiltered, so there may be some sediment left. This glass is led by sweetness with a little sourness at the tip of the tongue, so it goes superbly with the duck.

### Sakura
image: ./images/hajime/10.jpg | Grilled duck on a wide white plate with vegetables and orange sauce, Hajime, Osaka
On to dessert with Sakura, the symbol of spring, the start of life after a long winter. The chef serves flakes made from olive oil, sliced almonds, apricot, cheese and sakura sauce. The restaurant recommends eating one bite first and then pouring on the sakura sauce; the reason I won't reveal, you have to try it yourself. Then pour on the sauce, mix it in and eat. Personally, I think this dish has a very beautiful presentation, but the flavour is rather too sweet for me; both the sweetness of the sauce and the scent of the apricot overpower the aroma and taste of the ingredients a little too much. It isn't that this dish is not delicious, but from the presentation and the name of the dish, I expected the flavour to come out in another direction, not a deliciousness that comes from apricot.

We eat it with freshly squeezed apricot juice from France, which makes this dish even more apricot-forward.

A glass of Japanese apple cider, a complimentary glass from the restaurant's sommelier.

### Passion
image: ./images/hajime/11.jpg | A round yellow dessert on a grey stone, Hajime, Osaka
The next dessert is Passion, the first dish of Love, an important factor in creating new things. It is white chocolate filled with passion fruit juice mixed with vodka. I like this dish rather more than the previous one. The chef balances the white chocolate, the proportion of sweet-and-sour passion fruit juice and the mellow vodka superbly. Very delicious.

### Spread to the World
image: ./images/hajime/12.jpg | A round pink dessert with fresh strawberries and jelly, Hajime, Osaka
Spread to the world, the second dish of Love: vanilla ice cream served in a white chocolate ball, pecans, fresh strawberries and a kiss-shaped strawberry jelly, drizzled with a warm strawberry sauce. A tasty dessert; it is not too sweet, and the fresh sourness of the strawberry goes well with the cold sweetness of the vanilla. But I felt it was delicious without being as wow as the earlier dishes of this meal.

### Ginger ice cream (complimentary dish)
image: ./images/hajime/13.jpg | Ice cream and meringue on a square tray of clear ice, with an orange drink beside it, Hajime, Osaka
Our complimentary dish tonight turned out to be the dessert I liked most, a milk and ginger ice cream that was very delicious. The gentle spicy heat of the cold ice cream goes superbly with the sweet-and-sour yoghurt cream. The apricot adds a dimension of sweet-and-sour to the dish, before it is finished with a cherry meringue, a perfect close. Very delicious.

### Petitfour
image: ./images/hajime/14.jpg | Several small sweets arranged on a black stone slab, beside a large candy floss on a wooden stick, Hajime, Osaka
Now to the very last dish, Petitfour, excellent in every bite: a matcha tart with mascarpone cheese, an apple sphere, a popcorn-flavoured pudding, a caramel ball with a rich, sweet flavour, and a sweet-and-sour raspberry ice cream, except for the candy floss, which was so ordinary that I don't know why it was served. I saw every guest in the restaurant unable to finish it because they were so full.

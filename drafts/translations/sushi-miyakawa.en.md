Sushi Miyakawa was one of the restaurants I was most looking forward to on this trip. It is one of only a handful of sushi restaurants to hold three Michelin stars, and the only one on the island of Hokkaido. It also has the most reasonable prices among the three-star sushi restaurants (though, to be honest, it is still expensive ;)).

This review exists only thanks to a stroke of luck. I managed to get a seat, because Sushi Miyakawa books out extremely fast, with only 8 seats in the whole restaurant. But the luck might just as well be called bad luck, because the seat that was left fell on the very day I was due to stay at Jozankei with my tour group. After hesitating for a while, I thought, why not: I chose to have dinner at Miyakawa and then catch the bus to follow the group.

The weather in Hokkaido that day was pleasant, not too cold, with good sunshine. Backpack on my shoulder, I took the subway to Maruyama Koen station. The restaurant is not far from the station. I arrived at the door slightly early and was welcomed by a waitress who spoke reasonably good English. She took my winter gear and showed me to the restaurant's counter of just 8 seats.

While sipping a chilled Houbiden sake, I found myself thinking about the history of this man. Masaaki Miyakawa is a chef who has talent, and luck as well. He worked for many years alongside Masahiro Yoshitake of Sushi Yoshitake, the three-Michelin-star restaurant in Tokyo, until Yoshitake-san chose Miyakawa, his foremost disciple, to take the helm of the first branch of his new Sushi Yoshitake venture, Sushi Shikon. Chef Miyakawa did not let his master down: he built the restaurant up until it won three Michelin stars, before handing it on to the highly experienced Kakinuma-san, who still holds three Michelin stars to this day. After leaving Hong Kong, he came back to open Sushi Miyakawa in Sapporo and finally earned three Michelin stars again. That makes him one of the very few sushi chefs to have worked at three different three-Michelin-star sushi restaurants.

Besides the three Michelin stars, Sushi Miyakawa also holds a Tabelog Silver award with a score of 4.35, even higher than that of his master's restaurant, Sushi Yoshitake. It is one of the leading sushi restaurants not only in Hokkaido but in all of Japan.

Thanks to his experience abroad, Miyakawa-san is one of the few three-star sushi chefs who can communicate well in English, which makes the meal far more enjoyable for a foreign guest like me. Through the explanations of the chef and his young assistant, and the chatting I had with both of them, I found that although Miyakawa-san seems a quiet man, he turns out to have a real sense of humour once you talk to him. One more thing: the chef is rather serious about noise, so you should not take phone calls at his counter.

Chef Miyakawa's style is simple, flowing and steady, with a great deal of attention to detail. It may look plain, but he made time during this meal feel as though it were flying by. He seasons very little and puts the emphasis on superb ingredients. The rice is slightly salty with a gentle acidity, which brings out the sweet, firm grains in the mouth and suits the many white-fleshed fish served that night very well. Every nigiri was solid and excellent. The chef also has a masterful knife hand. Chef Miyakawa pays extreme attention to the temperature of the rice: over the course of the short meal he warmed it and topped it up more than four times, perhaps because the outside temperature is colder here than elsewhere, which makes him watch the temperature closely.

There was nothing seriously wrong with the food in this meal. The quality was excellent, and delicious enough to deserve three Michelin stars. If there is one thing I did not like, it is that the meal ended rather quickly: it took less than two hours. At first I thought there were few pieces because the serving was so quick and flowing, but looking at how much I had eaten, wow, it was not little at all. The other thing I did not like is that the restaurant does not serve dessert (some of you may like that, of course).

The service had no shortcomings either. A superb sake such as Houbiden was served at just the right chill, bringing out the sweetness and the beautiful fruity aroma of the sake. Another interesting thing about this restaurant is that the water served is iced water. At first it surprised me, and I wondered why they did not serve hot green tea like other famous sushi restaurants. But considering how cold it was outside (it snowed that day and the temperature was minus three degrees) and that the chef's sushi rice is served at a fairly cool temperature, I think cold water was probably the right choice.

Sushi Miyakawa serves only an omakase, priced at 20,000 yen. Have a look at the photos to see what was on the menu that day.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Shirako
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/01.jpg | Shirako soup with daikon in a ceramic bowl on a black saucer, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
The first dish was a shirako soup with daikon. The soup was light, with a hint of creaminess, and a very interesting opening. The choice of a soup, and of shirako in particular, may have been because the chef wanted to warm our bodies against the cold of Hokkaido.

### Kue
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/02.jpg | Thinly sliced kue sashimi sprinkled with chopped spring onion on a dotted plate, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Kue sashimi. The chef dressed this dish with just a touch of ponzu, a little sour and a little sweet, so as not to overwhelm the rich, sweet fattiness of this superb fish. The crisp, bouncy flesh has a truly distinctive texture. Excellent.

### Shime Saba
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/03.jpg | Lightly grilled smoked shime saba topped with wasabi in a ceramic bowl, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Next came smoked shime saba, lightly grilled over a gentle flame to crisp the skin. It was not heavily fatty, and the fish had no fishiness at all; you could eat it even without the spring onion and ginger. The best part was a bouncy crispness so pronounced that it hardly seemed like mackerel. The ginger and chopped spring onion on top, which the chef stresses a great deal, and the freshly shaped piece as a whole, gradually fell apart in the mouth. It was so good it nearly brought tears to my eyes. It would not be wrong to call it one of the best mackerel I have ever eaten.

### Seko Kani
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/04.jpg | Chilled boiled crab dressed in orange crab-roe sauce on a dark grey plate, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
The chef called this one queen crab; I am not sure whether it is seko crab or zuwai. He picks only females, boils them and serves them cold with black vinegar sauce, crab roe and yuzu zest. The crab was cooked beautifully, its flesh sweet with a touch of salt, set against the gently sour-sweet sauce with its distinctive, appetising aroma, and the scent of yuzu at the tip of the nose. The crab roe gave crunch, richness and a hint of the sea. For this dish I have to give ten out of ten.

### Ezo Awabi
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/05.jpg | Sliced ezo awabi in a creamy liver sauce in a dark green bowl, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Next the chef served ezo awabi from Hokkaido with a liver sauce. The abalone itself was cooked well enough, slightly crisp and chewy rather than purely soft. What I did not like was the sauce, creamy and fluffy, which I personally found too rich for abalone. There are several restaurants whose abalone impressed me more than this.

### Horseradish & Karasumi
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/06.jpg | Horseradish flower buds with karasumi on a pale blue round plate, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
This may have been the most surprising dish of the meal: the flower buds of horseradish with karasumi, dried bonito and orange zest. At the first bite I was rather surprised, because the karasumi was fairly mild but cured very well, so it did not overpower the fragrance of yuzu zest or the gentle sweetness of the horseradish buds. Delicious in a bewildering sort of way.

### Tai
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/07.jpg | Tai sushi on a dotted ceramic plate beside pickled ginger, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Then the sushi began, with tai as the first piece. The flesh was firm and very rich, with no fishiness, and a faint fragrance of rice, with the sudachi lingering on the tip of the tongue. Very good indeed.

### Akami
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/08.jpg | Akami sushi marinated in deep red soy sauce beside pickled ginger, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Next was akami marinated in soy sauce. The flesh was threaded with fine sinew, giving a crunchy, toothsome feel that was quite distinctive. The soy sauce used for the zuke was of very high quality and, blended with kombu, gave a slight sweetness and umami. Miyakawa's firm, crisp rice contrasted with the bouncy bite of the fish. It was a superb akami zuke, delicious and with a style all its own. This piece impressed me greatly.

### Kinmedai
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/09.jpg | Pale pink kinmedai sushi on a dotted ceramic plate, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
This kinmedai was one of the best I have ever eaten. The chef brushed on the thinnest film of soy sauce, which lets you perceive the superb quality of the fish. Kinmedai is usually crisp and bouncy, but Miyakawa's was very soft. The flesh had a slight richness, set against the crispness of the rice and its faint fragrance. Very impressive.

### Chutoro
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/10.jpg | Lightly grilled soy-marinated chutoro sushi on a dotted plate, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
The chef uses tuna caught around Hokkaido, from the fish market at Hakodate, weighing only a little over 200 kilos. The next piece he served was chutoro, marinated in soy sauce and lightly grilled. It had a faint aroma of wood smoke and a more intense flavour than the akami. The fish had crunchy sinew like the akami, but the flesh was fattier. Excellent. Miyakawa's pickled ginger is fairly crisp and salty.

### Kohada
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/11.jpg | Shiny silver-skinned kohada sushi on a ceramic plate beside pickled ginger, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Next the chef served kohada, a piece I normally do not care for very much. But Miyakawa's kohada was wonderfully sweet, the flesh sweeter than it was fatty. It was a piece that opened my eyes.

### Sawara
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/12.jpg | Smoked sawara sushi sprinkled with yuzu zest on a dotted plate, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Sawara, or Spanish mackerel, came next. The chef smokes sawara from Spain and sprinkles it with grated yuzu zest before serving. It smelled of smoke, with a faint note of yuzu citrus at the tip of the nose. The fatty flesh had a sweetness that lingered on the tongue, contrasting nicely with the slightly warm rice. Delicious.

### Shima Aji
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/13.jpg | Shima aji sushi topped with spring onion on a ceramic plate, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Shima aji from Shizuoka. Fairly crisp and rich, not very fishy, with a faint fragrance of ginger and spring onion.

### Takuan
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/14.jpg | Two slices of pale takuan on a dotted ceramic plate, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
The takuan was paler than usual. It was crunchier than at other restaurants and milder in flavour, with a sweetness that lingered on the tip of the tongue. A refreshing piece.

### Uni
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/15.jpg | Bafun uni on rice in a small bowl on a black saucer, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Then the uni. This piece was rice mixed with uni sauce, topped with kombu-cured bafun uni and seaweed, with the chef using uni from Hakodate throughout, as before. I was slightly disappointed by this one, because the rice was a touch too cold (only a touch, really) and so sour that it stood out and did not blend with the uni. Otherwise it was good: the sweetness of the superb uni balanced well with the seaweed. Delicious, though not to the fullest.

### Kuruma Ebi
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/16.jpg | Half-cooked kuruma ebi sushi on a dotted ceramic plate, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Now one of my favourite sushi of the meal, kuruma ebi. The chef served the prawn half cooked, half raw. As it went in, the sweet, pungent wasabi came first, followed by the superb sweet, crisp and bouncy flesh of the prawn. In this piece the chef does not rely on the richness of the prawn's fat, which lets the flavour of the flesh stand out sharply. The wasabi, slightly more generous than in the other pieces, brought out the prawn's sweetness all the more. Truly superb.

### Anago
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/17.jpg | Grilled anago sushi glazed with sweet sauce on a ceramic plate beside pickled ginger, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
We closed with anago. It was very soft, with a lightly charred crisp skin and a slightly earthy note. It was not very fatty, and went with the gently sweet sauce with its faint hint of yuzu zest. Another very good piece.

### Miso Soup
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/18.jpg | Miso soup scented with crab and sprinkled with spring onion in a black wooden bowl, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Miyakawa's miso soup uses a crab stock and white miso. It smelled wonderfully of crab, but personally I found the flavour a little thin.

### Tamagoyaki
image: ./images/sushi-miyakawa/19.jpg | Two thick pieces of tamagoyaki on a ceramic plate beside pickled ginger, Sushi Miyakawa, Sapporo
Tamagoyaki, or sweet omelette. Good and distinctive, though not quite a wow. It carries a crab fragrance, which really is a style of its own.

Today's review is a Top 10 restaurant in Japan, one of the finest kaiseki restaurants, the pinnacle of washoku and a legend of Kyoto cuisine: Ogata Honten. Ta-da!

I booked my flight because of this restaurant. To be honest, I could not get a table at Noma Kyoto, but Ogata gave me a seat, so since I was going anyway I crammed some other restaurants in, and it became yet another eating trip, even though I had only just returned from Tokyo a week before. As for how superb Ogata is: it is one of the highest-scoring restaurants on Tabelog, with a score of 4.58, plus OAD Top 100 Asia and 2 Michelin stars.

Toshiro Ogata began his career as a chef back in high school, when he took a part-time job at a ryokan in Kyoto. There he was invited to join Wakuden, a legendary Kyoto restaurant that has trained many top talents. He spent more than 13 years at Wakuden before finally leaving to open his own restaurant, and Ogata-san's last position was head chef at Wakuden.

I have to admit that Chef Ogata's food has a truly distinctive style of its own and is executed superbly. The food here is fluid and gentle, with components built more from simmering, steaming or water than in ordinary kaiseki, though the grilled dishes are done without any shortfall either. What seemed to me weaker than the other dishes was the fried course, which lacked some distinction and character. The seasoning is minimal yet powerful, emphasising natural pairings of ingredients to draw out their deliciousness. Overall, this was beyond doubt one of the most delicious and most distinctive meals I have ever eaten in Japan.

As for service, it can be a little slow because the staff seemed short-handed. The chef is a serious man who does not speak English, yet underneath he has quite a lot of jokes and humour. He seems fairly open and welcoming to foreigners. I recommend ordering sake, as Chef Ogata picks a glass for each person from the restaurant's collection of antique glasses to suit their personality. Let's see what was in this meal.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Fuki
image: ./images/ogata/01.jpg | Pale green strands curled into shreds on a white plate on a black tray, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
The first dish was fuki, or Japanese butterbur, simmered in dashi and served with warm shari rice. The rice was not very soft but slightly crunchy, lightly seasoned, gently sour and sweet. The vegetable was cooked in dashi, simmered until crisp yet with intense umami. The distinctive fragrance and gentle bitterness of the vegetable, together with the sweet-sour rice, gently stimulated the appetite. Very delicious.

### Onidaruma Okoze
image: ./images/ogata/02.jpg | Stonefish sashimi and its various parts on a dotted glass plate, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
Onidaruma okoze, or stonefish, is a prized ingredient, but this fish has venom around its dorsal fin, so eating it raw is very risky unless the cook is skilled, especially around the skin. Chef Ogata chose to serve it as stonefish sashimi with ponzu sauce and daikon oroshi, with several parts: skin, flesh, fin, roe and liver. The flesh was very crisp, but what I liked more was the chewy skin and the fin, firm and crunchy. A strange, interesting texture. It went well with the sweet-sour sauce. I noticed that the ponzu here was spicier than usual.

### Katsuo (Bonito)
image: ./images/ogata/03.jpg | Sliced grilled katsuo beside onion on a gold plate set against a small blue-and-white plate, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
The next dish was katsuo, served with sesame oil, shin tamanegi onion and Kyoto spring onion. It looked very simple, but every component was excellent, so it came out well. The restaurant's katsuo was very soft, with little of the iron smell characteristic of the fish, yet full of flavour and umami, a very good piece. The sesame oil here was very fragrant, with a distinctive aroma; I suspect it is house-made. The Kyoto spring onion, pickled, was pungent with a light sweet-sour, and the freshly sliced onion, sweet and pungent, was delicious and added dimension to the dish.

### Takenoko Water
image: ./images/ogata/04.jpg | A rough stone cup holding a clear broth for drinking, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
This little cup is the water from steamed takenoko, squeezed out of the husks. This month I have eaten so much takenoko that I am sick of it, and this restaurant is what I would call beyond, but it was the most impressive: intense, very sweet, with so much umami, more intense and rich than the takenoko one usually eats.

### Simmered Takenoko
image: ./images/ogata/05.jpg | Simmered takenoko in a black bowl beside wakame in a small cup, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
Simmered takenoko (again; do not try this at home, eating kaiseki back to back like this), which Ogata served with shin wakame.

But what Ogata makes is the best I have eaten. The dashi, though not very intense, was complex and rounded, never competing with either the seaweed or the bamboo shoot. The crispness, the sweetness and the distinctive fragrance of the takenoko were very good. I recommend trying it plain and then mixed with the seaweed; the flavour changes. This must be the best simmered bamboo shoot I have eaten.

### Uni with Tempura Nori
image: ./images/ogata/06.jpg | Orange uni on seaweed tempura on a brightly coloured plate, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
Uni with seaweed tempura. This bite was umami to the extreme. If the seaweed had absorbed less oil, it would surely have been better.

### Fried Asparagus
image: ./images/ogata/07.jpg | A hand holding battered fried asparagus, pale green, wrapped in white paper, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
Fried asparagus. Very good ingredient: crisp, large, juicy, sweet, with no sharp greenness. Served hot. Delicious.

### Grilled Takenoko
image: ./images/ogata/08.jpg | Grilled takenoko on a white crackle-glazed ceramic plate, scattered with green leaves, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
Takenoko grilled right there, fresh. The fragrance was truly wonderful, the scent of soy sauce and the distinctive aroma of takenoko drifting into the nose. We ate it with fresh sansho leaves, whose distinctive spiciness brings out the sweetness of the takenoko, lifted by a thin brush of soy sauce, and the salt added on top brings out the sweetness even further. Superb.

### Beef Course
image: ./images/ogata/09.jpg | Sliced wagyu in sauce sprinkled with green sansho in a blue circle-patterned bowl, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
The next dish was a beef course. The chef took bonito-scented dashi and simmered it with fresh sansho, then dipped it briefly with top-grade wagyu fillet. The slight sweetness in the soup went interestingly with the distinctive flavour of the sansho. The beef, not very fatty and fragrant, kept the dish from being too heavy. The sweet scent of thin citrus zest brought all the aromas together. A dish that looks simple, yet every ingredient is balanced and settled. Very delicious.

### Clam Rice
image: ./images/ogata/10.jpg | Clam rice in a small white bowl, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
The rice dish today was rice cooked with clams, large clams, with kisu tempura. A dish that looks outrageously simple but is no ordinary one. Although the rice looks moist, it is not mushy at all and has a slight crunch, which is probably the chef's style. The tempura had a light peppery heat that brought out the sweetness of the clams and the fish perfectly. Very good.

### Tsukemono
image: ./images/ogata/11.jpg | Brown tsukemono with grated daikon on a round ceramic plate, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
Today's tsukemono came in a variety of flavours: sweet-salty lotus root, Chinese flowers with the heat leading, and sour daikon.

### Soba
image: ./images/ogata/12.jpg | Soba noodles laid out on a blue-and-white plate with a cup of dark dipping sauce, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
The soba was probably the dish I liked least today. It is not that it was not delicious, but it came out middling. The noodles were very springy, but did not smell much of buckwheat flour.

### Warabimochi
image: ./images/ogata/13.jpg | Warabimochi dusted with brown roasted soybean powder on a ceramic plate, Ogata Honten, Kyoto
The dessert was warabimochi, wonderfully smooth going down. I felt it went very well with the food today.

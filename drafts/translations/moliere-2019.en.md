"Straight off the plane and into dinner" is hardly an exaggeration for today's schedule. The moment EVA Air flight BR115 touched down at Shin Chitose, I hurried through immigration and took the bus into Sapporo. By the time I had collected my bag and checked in, it was nearly five o'clock. I was in a rush because I had arranged to meet a friend late that night for yakiniku at the highest-rated beef restaurant on Tabelog in all of Hokkaido, so the first target of this trip had to be booked for six o'clock. That meant a quick change of clothes and straight out to today's destination, Molière.

Molière sits right beside Maruyama Park, quite a way from my hotel in the Susukino area. I stepped out into the cold air, and the snow that had begun to fall in the evening made everything even more bitter. I took the subway to Maruyama-koen station, then walked past the US Consulate. After about fifteen minutes on foot, I arrived at the first restaurant of the trip.

Molière is a long-established, famous French restaurant in Sapporo with a long history, regarded as the first Western fine dining restaurant on the island of Hokkaido. It is recognised internationally with the exalted three Michelin stars from the Michelin Guide. It is not only foreigners who rate it: Molière also scores 4.28 on Tabelog, placing it in the top ten in Hokkaido. The owner-chef, Hiroshi Nakamichi, was even once appointed as a food adviser to the G8 summit in Hokkaido.

The service team at Molière lived up to the standard of a three-Michelin-star restaurant in every respect: attentive to detail, observant, warm and friendly. Communication is especially easy for foreign guests, because the staff speak better English than at most restaurants in Japan. They looked after me superbly. When a plate dropped in the kitchen with a loud crash, they took turns coming to apologise three or four times. The waiter tried to make conversation with me based on the background in my booking history, which means the team works hard and researches its guests in order to give each of them the right service. I admire this very much. This is service worthy of the world's leading restaurants.

Today I had a second restaurant booked, the highest-rated beef yakiniku in Sapporo on Tabelog (that review will have to wait), so I had to ask for each course to be served faster than usual. I asked the maître d' how long the meal would take, and he worked out the timing, speeding up the meal and adding the travel time so that I would reach my next appointment on schedule (his calculation was spot on). My heartfelt thanks for that. I sat down three courses behind the table next to me, yet finished before them. Ha!

The waiters came by regularly to ask whether the portions were enough, whether I was full, and whether they should reduce the size of the next plate since I had a second meal ahead. Whenever I showed particular enthusiasm for a dish, they would promptly bring me an extra helping. And when I left something unfinished, they asked whether anything was wrong. This, too, shows the highest level of professionalism and the seasoned experience of Molière's front-of-house team.

The cooking at Molière is classic French blended with contemporary dishes, distinguished by superb Hokkaido ingredients and freshly prepared food that makes the most of outstanding local produce. The menu consists of set courses only, starting at just 4,600 yen at lunch and rising to 16,000 yen at dinner, which makes Molière the cheapest three-Michelin-star restaurant in the world. The lunch menu is considerably cheaper and suits anyone wanting to try something new, but dinner is still affordable and easy to book. If I have the chance to return to Sapporo, this restaurant will certainly remain one of my choices. In fact, I originally rated its value for money at 9.75, but the drinks prices, where the pairing can come close to the cost of the food, made me lower the score.

Today I chose the Saison menu. Let's see what it holds.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Onion Tart
image: ./images/moliere-2019/01.jpg | A golden onion tart on a cream ceramic plate, Molière, Sapporo
The first bite is a canapé in the form of an onion tart.

The waiter suggested we eat it with our hands, but with its fairly large size and runny filling, I had a bit of a struggle.

It arrived warm, with an appetising aroma of butter and onion. It may be a slightly heavy way to start, but with the bitter cold outside, it is a very good opener. As for the taste, it is delicious indeed: a thin pastry shell with a soft, warm, creamy filling and the mouth-watering fragrance of onion. That gentle warmth makes the heart swell in the freezing air of Hokkaido.

### Shiitake Consommé
image: ./images/moliere-2019/02.jpg | Shiitake mushroom soup with white onion foam in a wide white bowl, Molière, Sapporo
The next dish suited the snow falling that night perfectly. It looks very simple, but it may well be the most delicious mushroom soup of my life.

A shiitake consommé, topped with an onion-flavoured foam. It may look plain, yet it is wonderfully delicious.

This dish impressed me enormously. It was served hot but not scalding, with a Western aroma from the stock and the onion, and an Eastern undertone from the shiitake. The dried shiitake sprinkled on top is crunchy, adding texture and umami to a soup that is perfectly salted and gently sweet.

The onion foam does more than add fragrance: it adds another dimension of texture, so the soup never grows dull and becomes all the more interesting. Superb.

### Scallop Tempura
image: ./images/moliere-2019/03.jpg | A hand holding crisp, deep-fried shiso leaf tempura, Molière, Sapporo
The next dish was one of the most impressive of the meal: scallop tempura with ōba (shiso leaf). At first I was rather puzzled that the chef served tempura, but one bite and I understood how superb it is. This bite may seem salty to me because of the quite generous amount of salt sprinkled on, but only when I bit through to the scallop in the centre did that sharp saltiness, which had seemed too much, wonderfully heighten the sweetness flooding out of the juicy scallop. The crunchy ōba wrapper matches the bouncy, perfectly cooked flesh beautifully.

This dish is superb, showing technique so excellent that it could be served in a Michelin-starred tempura restaurant without any embarrassment.

### Vegetable Salad
image: ./images/moliere-2019/04.jpg | A salad of assorted vegetables on a white plate, dressed with orange sauce and sesame, Molière, Sapporo
Next the chef served a vegetable salad, made with a variety of vegetables, with a sesame sauce, plum, beetroot, spinach and lemon.

The waiter advised mixing everything together before eating. Everything here is done by the book: fresh vegetables cooked in a variety of ways, prepared perfectly. But I found it ordinary and lacking much dimension in flavour. The creamy sesame sauce is too heavy and rich, and it completely smothers the natural deliciousness of these otherwise excellent vegetables. The difference is especially clear when compared with Chikyu, the signature dish of the Osaka restaurant Hajime.

### Fried Oyster
image: ./images/moliere-2019/05.jpg | Crisp fried oysters beside tartare sauce and a yellow sauce on a white plate, Molière, Sapporo
This one was done well too: fried oysters with house-made mayonnaise, tartare sauce and egg yolk, finished with a sprinkling of curry powder.

For this dish, we had to mix everything together before eating. It is one of the best oyster dishes I have ever had. The oysters have a wafer-thin coating, crisp outside and juicy and sweet within, matching the fragrance of the curry perfectly. The sour richness of the tartare sauce and mayonnaise mixed in heightens the sweet aroma of the superb oysters beautifully. A simple, balanced dish that comes together perfectly.

### Tile Fish
image: ./images/moliere-2019/06.jpg | Crisp-skinned grilled fish with lemon butter sauce and small potatoes, Molière, Sapporo
Next came Hokkaido tilefish, served with a butter and lemon sauce. The fish arrived on a hot plate, and the aroma of excellent butter wafted out, stirring curiosity, since the scent had drifted out from the kitchen. This dish is done by the book: the flesh is bouncy and juicy, not overcooked. The standout is the croûtons, whose lemony sourness cuts through the richness of the butter sharply. Another dish that is cooked well, though personally I found it a little too much.

### Lemon Tea Sorbet
image: ./images/moliere-2019/07.jpg | Lemon tea sorbet being drenched with brandy in a clear glass, Molière, Sapporo
We cleansed the palate with a lemon tea sorbet and pear brandy. The taste of this dish is hard to describe; it is rather strange.

The slight sourness and astringency of the tea, and the fiery, fragrant sweetness of the pear brandy: all the elements come together in a baffling way. I like it, but anyone who dislikes the astringency of tea may not.

### Beef Cheek
image: ./images/moliere-2019/08.jpg | Braised beef cheek in a dark red wine sauce, topped with white crisps, Molière, Sapporo
Now to our meat course, in which the chef braised Hokkaido beef cheek in a red wine sauce, to be eaten with mustard and horseradish.

On its own, the wine-braised beef cheek is up to the standard of a restaurant of this level: tender, fragrant with red wine, with a slight richness of beef. But what sets this dish apart is the Hokkaido horseradish shaved in ribbons on top. Its sweet, pungent aroma adds real dimension to the braised cheek, giving the dish a faint Japanese touch that makes it different.

The potato gratin served alongside is good, but nothing to wow.

### Hokkaido Blue Cheese
image: ./images/moliere-2019/09.jpg | Hokkaido blue cheese on rye bread on a long wooden tray, Molière, Sapporo
The Hokkaido blue cheese was served warm on rye bread with walnuts. For this dish I asked the waiter for half the normal portion.

Although it is called blue cheese, this one is not strong in aroma, so even people who dislike blue cheese can eat it. It has a distinctive earthy smell that I did not much like, even though I can usually enjoy blue cheese with great relish, but it was a fun thing to try.

### Mandarin Sherbet
image: ./images/moliere-2019/10.jpg | Mandarin sorbet served in a small orange shell in a porcelain cup, Molière, Sapporo
Now on to dessert. The first is a mandarin sherbet. It smells of green citrus and is not very sweet. It looks ordinary, but the green citrus aroma and the gentle sweetness are simply delicious.

### Mont Blanc
image: ./images/moliere-2019/11.jpg | Finely piped chestnut Mont Blanc on a wide white plate, Molière, Sapporo
Chestnut Mont Blanc. This one is not very sweet, with meringue and fresh cream in the centre cutting against the fragrance of the chestnut. It is done quite well, but I have had better Mont Blancs in many places.

### Petit Four
image: ./images/moliere-2019/12.jpg | Puffy pastries dusted with icing sugar on a round wooden tin, Molière, Sapporo
Our petit four is a French-style baked pastry that tastes like a doughnut. This was well made, with a tart lemon jelly and fresh strawberry. Overall it is a little ordinary, but the pastry itself is good. My only complaint is that the piece was far too big for me.

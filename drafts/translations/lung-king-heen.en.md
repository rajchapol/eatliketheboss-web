Lung King Heen means "Dragon View", a rather audacious name for a restaurant, yet one this place wears with every right. It was not only the first Chinese restaurant in the world to earn three Michelin stars; it has collected a great many other honours too, among them Forbes and CNN recognition and a place among the world's best restaurants. Lung King Heen is an essential standard for Chinese cooking in Hong Kong and across the world, the benchmark everyone measures against.

That evening my travelling companions and I walked, unhurried, through the sticky heat of a Hong Kong July, from Hong Kong Station to the IFC Mall, cutting through into the Four Seasons Hotel, home to this celebrated restaurant. After being pointed the way by an impeccably English-speaking Hong Kong concierge in the lobby, I stepped into the lift and rode up to the restaurant.

Lung King Heen is one of only two Chinese restaurants in the world to hold three Michelin stars (2017), together with Eight in Macau, and one of only two Chinese restaurants on The Asia's 50 Best Restaurants list (2018). When it first received three stars in the very first Hong Kong Michelin Guide, Michelin sent inspectors to the restaurant twelve times in a single year!!! That is far above the usual three visits. (At that time Lung King Heen was a Chinese three-Michelin-star restaurant in the world.)

Lung King Heen is one of my regular restaurants, one I always drop in on whenever I am in Hong Kong, because this legendary restaurant offers a superb view, even better service, and food of a still finer standard: dim sum made and steamed fresh, basket by basket, to order; fresh ideas in Chinese cooking that have sparked chefs around the world; and prices that lead me to dare call it "the cheapest three-Michelin-star restaurant in the world".

You should book Lung King Heen at least a month ahead to be sure of a table. The Michelin Guide even wrote about how hard it is to book in its 2017 edition ("Your first and only challenge will be attempting to secure a reservation at this most popular of restaurants").

The service at Lung King Heen is still first class, even compared with restaurants of the same calibre, yet this time I felt that some of the charm and the standard had slipped a little. The menu lacked change and, with it, colour, perhaps because I have now visited more than six times. Even so, there is no denying that Lung King Heen remains one of the finest Chinese restaurants in the world.

From all of this, you will surely gather that I am not the only one who has fallen in love with Lung King Heen.

## The art of taste in this meal

### Compliments: Fried Shrimp and Squid Balls
image: ./images/lung-king-heen/01.jpg | Fried shrimp and squid balls sprinkled with almonds and served with orange sauce, a nibble at Lung King Heen, Hong Kong
The first plate was the restaurant's complimentary nibble: still the fried shrimp and squid balls with almonds, sprinkled with salt and almond flakes and served with orange sauce, just as it was four or five years ago. The springy, bouncy balls pair well with the crisp saltiness of the almonds, cut by the faint sourness of the orange sauce. Nothing spectacular for me.

### Barbecued Pork
image: ./images/lung-king-heen/02.jpg | Glossy red barbecued pork on a blue ceramic plate, Lung King Heen, Hong Kong
The first proper dish was barbecued pork. The restaurant does not use the outrageously expensive Spanish Ibérico black pork that other restaurants do, but I actually prefer it, because the flavour sits in perfect balance. It is not just rich and fatty: there is saltiness from Chinese wine and salt, sweetness from honey, a faint sourness from vinegar, and the fragrance of a variety of spices in well-judged proportion.

### Asparagus in Bean Curd Skin
image: ./images/lung-king-heen/03.jpg | Asparagus wrapped in bean curd skin, served with wood ear mushrooms and house-made tofu, Lung King Heen, Hong Kong
The next dish may taste a little bland to Thai palates, perhaps because the chef does not want the seasoning to overpower the deliciousness of the vegetables. It was asparagus wrapped in bean curd skin, eaten with wood ear mushrooms and house-made tofu. The tofu was sweet, fragrant and delicious, every bit the equal of famous tofu shops in Japan, and the vegetable was wonderfully sweet and crisp.

### Prawns with Black Garlic
image: ./images/lung-king-heen/04.jpg | Prawns stir-fried with black garlic, chilli and onion, Lung King Heen, Hong Kong
The next dish is a favourite of mine: prawns stir-fried with organic black garlic. The sweet-sour taste and fragrance of the black garlic go well with the large prawns, springy and crisp yet, astonishingly, still holding the sweetness of the flesh. The heat of dried chilli and the coriander cut through beautifully, so the dish is never too heavy or cloying.

### Wok-fried Beef Tenderloin with Foie Gras
image: ./images/lung-king-heen/05.jpg | Wok-fried beef tenderloin with foie gras and spring onion, Lung King Heen, Hong Kong
Beef tenderloin wok-fried with foie gras, ginger and spring onion. The beef was superbly tender and melted in the mouth. The foie gras came as handsome, intact pieces that did not melt away, not overly rich, with the richness cut by ginger, but personally I would have liked more foie gras aroma in this dish.

### Complimentary Sweets: Custard Pastry and Chrysanthemum Jelly
image: ./images/lung-king-heen/06.jpg | Egg custard pastry and chrysanthemum jelly, dessert at Lung King Heen, Hong Kong
A complimentary sweet from the restaurant: a cream-filled pastry that was sooooo delicious. The pastry was thin and crisp in layers, the filling not too dry, fragrant with egg and vanilla. The other was chrysanthemum jelly with Job's tears, which was also quite good.
